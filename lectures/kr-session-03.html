Part one of three · 25 minutes

Description logic

You already have first-order logic. Today's question is not what description logic can say — it is what it deliberately gives up, and what you get in exchange.

The bargain

A decidable fragment, bought by restricting the language

Description logic is a family of fragments of first-order logic restricted to unary and binary predicates, with no explicit variables. That restriction is the whole point: it buys back decidability, and with it a reasoner that terminates.

  • A holds axioms about classes and properties.
  • An holds assertions about individuals.
  • Concept and role constructors replace quantified formulae: intersection, union, complement, existential and universal restriction, cardinality.
The exchange rate

Full first-order logic is undecidable, so no reasoner can be both complete and guaranteed to halt. Every DL you meet is a point on that trade curve — and OWL 2's profiles are three named points on it.

# First-order logic
∀x ( Shipment(x) → ∃y ( hasCarrier(x,y) ∧ Carrier(y) ) )

# Description logic (no variables)
Shipment ⊑ ∃hasCarrier.Carrier

# OWL 2, Turtle
:Shipment rdfs:subClassOf [
    a owl:Restriction ;
    owl:onProperty :hasCarrier ;
    owl:someValuesFrom :Carrier
] .

Because it makes the axioms composable objects rather than sentences. A tool can compute the subsumption hierarchy of a set of DL axioms; it cannot do the same for arbitrary FOL sentences. Every Protégé feature you will use in the lab — the inferred hierarchy, the unsatisfiable-class highlight, the explanation pane — exists because of that restriction.

What a reasoner actually does for you

Three standard tasks, three different bugs they find

01 · Consistency

Can this ontology have any model at all?

An inconsistent ontology entails everything, which makes every downstream answer worthless. Usually caused by a disjointness axiom colliding with a cardinality one.

Finds: contradictory axioms.

02 · Classification

What is a subclass of what, once you take the axioms seriously?

The inferred hierarchy is the single most useful diagnostic in the lab. Read it as a claim about your intent and check whether you meant it.

Finds: unintended subsumptions and equivalences.

03 · Instance realisation

Which classes does this individual belong to?

This is where the ontology earns money: a shipment gets classified as AtRiskShipment without anyone writing a rule that says so.

Finds: mis-modelled restrictions on real data.

A consistent ontology with a clean red-free hierarchy can still be wrong, and this is the common case. If you declare hasCarrier with domain Shipment and then assert purchaseOrder/88 :hasCarrier carrier/DHL, no reasoner complains — it quietly infers that the purchase order is a shipment. Under the open world assumption that is a valid entailment, not an error.

This is why Session 4 exists. Catching it needs closed-world validation, which is SHACL's job, not OWL's. Today, notice the pattern; next week, make it fail a build.

Check question

You modelled DelayedShipment and AtRiskShipment as separate classes. The reasoner reports them as equivalent. Which reasoning task surfaced that?

Read the hierarchy as a claim

Every inferred line is the reasoner telling you what you said. When it disagrees with what you meant, the model is wrong — not the reasoner.

Part two of three · 25 minutes

OWL 2 and its profiles

Syntax over the semantics we just fixed — and one engineering decision that determines whether reasoning finishes on a graph of realistic size.

Vocabulary

Five constructs carry most enterprise modelling

owl:someValuesFrom
Existential restriction. Every shipment has some carrier.
owl:allValuesFrom
Universal restriction. All parts of this assembly are certified parts. Weaker than it looks: it says nothing about existence.
owl:disjointWith
No individual is in both. The axiom that makes contradictions findable.
owl:inverseOf
Gives you the reverse traversal for free instead of maintaining two properties.
owl:cardinality
Qualified counting. The construct most likely to push you out of the EL profile.
Two traps, both common in submissions

Domain and range are not constraints. They are entailment rules; asserting one lets the reasoner classify anything you attach. is not a fuzzy match. It is a logical identity and the reasoner will propagate it everywhere.

@prefix iof: <https://spec.industrialontologies.org/ontology/core/Core/> .
@prefix gs1: <https://gs1.org/voc/> .
@prefix ul:  <https://ul.edu.lb/kr/scm#> .

# Reuse first: subclass a published class.
ul:LateShipment a owl:Class ;
  rdfs:subClassOf iof:MaterialTransport ;
  rdfs:subClassOf [
     a owl:Restriction ;
     owl:onProperty ul:deliveredAfter ;
     owl:someValuesFrom ul:CommittedDate ] ;
  skos:definition "A transport whose actual delivery
     follows the committed delivery date." ;
  # CQ annotation — graded. See slide 15.
  ul:answersCQ "Which shipments arrived late last quarter?" .

ul:LateShipment owl:disjointWith ul:CancelledShipment .
ul:deliveredAfter owl:inverseOf ul:committedBefore .

Every class in your submission needs the skos:definition and at least one ul:answersCQ. A class with neither has not earned its place.

Profiles as an engineering decision

Drag the seam. Same intent, different profile, different guarantee.

ul:AtRiskShipment ⊑ ul:Shipment
  ⊓ ∃ul:handledBy.ul:SanctionedCarrier


OWL 2 EL · ELK · polynomial time
ul:AtRiskShipment ⊑ ul:Shipment
  ⊓ ∀ul:handledBy.ul:SanctionedCarrier
  ⊓ ≥2 ul:handledBy.ul:Carrier
  ⊓ ¬ul:InsuredShipment
OWL 2 DL · HermiT · exponential worst case
Stays in OWL 2 EL
  • Classification in polynomial time. ELK returns on millions of axioms.
  • Existential restrictions, intersection, class hierarchies, property chains.
  • The profile the biomedical world runs at scale, and the safe default here.
Leaves EL the moment you add these
  • Universal restriction, cardinality and negation are all outside EL.
  • You now need HermiT, and worst-case runtime is exponential.
  • It may still finish on your graph. “May” is not a property you can deploy on.
Declare your profile in the report

ROBOT will tell you which profile you are in. If it is not the one you intended, that is a finding, not a formality — and the Session 8 defence will ask why.

Part three of three · 15 minutes

Reuse, or build your own private vocabulary nobody can read

Industrial ontologies already exist for supply chain. Not using them is a decision, and it is one you have to justify.

Where your classes attach

Four layers above you, two alignments beside you

  • BFO 2020ISO/IEC 21838-2 foundational ontology. Continuant against occurrent, and little else you touch directly.
  • IOF CoreIndustrial Ontologies Foundry mid-level: material entities, processes, information content.
  • IOF SCROSupply Chain Reference Ontology. Your starting file in the lab, already loaded in the workspace.
  • ul:… your extensionOnly the classes your competency questions demand and SCRO does not already provide.

Alignment hazard

Aligning outward with owl:equivalentClass imports every axiom on the other side, including ones you have not read. Prefer rdfs:subClassOf until you can defend the equivalence.

Method · drag or use the arrow buttons

Put the method in the order you will actually work in the lab.

  • Extend SCRO only where a CQ needs a class it lacks
  • Write the competency questions the organisation actually asks
  • Read the inferred hierarchy and fix the model, not the output
  • Search for a published ontology that already answers them
  • Run ELK, then HermiT
  • Run ROBOT, record the OWL profile and the quality report
The rule that decides scope

A class earns its place by answering a question the organisation actually asks. If you cannot name the question, delete the class.

  1. Which shipments arrived after their committed date last quarter, by carrier?
  2. Which plants can legally serve port X, and which of those served it in practice?
  3. If supplier S fails, which finished goods lose their only source of a component?
  4. Which freight rate band applies to a consignment of weight W on lane L?

You need eight, recorded in the ontology as ul:answersCQ annotations. Four are given; four are yours.

Hands-on lab · 95 minutes · starts now

Build ontology v1 on IOF SCRO

  1. Open the provided Protégé workspace with IOF SCRO already loaded. Do not start from an empty file.
  2. Write eight competency questions and record each as a ul:answersCQ annotation.
  3. Extend SCRO with the classes and properties your Session 1 constraint inventory demands, reusing GS1 Web Vocabulary terms where they fit.
  4. Run ELK, then HermiT. Read the inferred hierarchy for unsatisfiable classes, unintended equivalences and subsumptions you did not mean.
  5. Fix the model, not the reasoner output. Re-run until the hierarchy matches your intent.
  6. Run ROBOT from the command line for a quality report, and record which OWL profile you are in.
  7. Ten-minute licensing exercise — see the next slide.

Inside the lab · 10 minutes

Licence literacy is part of the subject, not an administrative detail

  1. Resolve the SCORVoc PURL live and inspect the vocabulary.
  2. Find the ODC PDDL dedication in the repository.
  3. Find the all rights reserved notice in the same repository.
  4. Decide which one governs, and write down why in two sentences.

Because it is the honest reason the course builds on IOF SCRO rather than SCORVoc, and because every third-party ontology, vocabulary and dataset in your final report must be listed with its licence. A vocabulary with two contradictory licence statements is not a hypothetical — it is the first one a supply-chain search turns up.

The transferable skill: when the licence is ambiguous, the ambiguity is the finding. Record it and choose differently.

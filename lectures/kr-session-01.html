Part one of three · 18 minutes

Meaning is not in the schema

You already know how to store this data. The question today is where its meaning went, and why nobody noticed it leaving.

The distinction the course is built on

A schema records shape. It does not record what anything means.

A column type says a value is a string of at most 20 characters. It does not say the string names a legal entity, that two of them may be the same company, or that one of them stopped existing in 2019.

  • Meaning lives in three places, none machine-readable: four people's heads, a stale wiki page, and 300 report WHERE clauses.
  • A is that knowledge written down where a program can reach it.
Trap

"We documented it" is not the same as representing it. A PDF data dictionary is a description of meaning. An ontology is meaning a machine can act on, and the difference is whether a reasoner can use it.

-- Everything the database knows:
CREATE TABLE supplier (
  vendor_id   VARCHAR(20) PRIMARY KEY,
  name        VARCHAR(120),
  country     CHAR(2),
  status      CHAR(1)
);

-- What a human knows and it does not:
--   'X' is a merged duplicate, not a deletion
--   country is the billing one, not the plant
--   vendor and manufacturer differ, and this
--     table quietly holds both

Billing against plant becomes two properties (Session 3). The merged-duplicate rule becomes a SHACL shape that fails a build (Session 4). Vendor against manufacturer becomes entity resolution with a reported error rate (Session 5). The course is taking those comments out of people's heads and making them execute.

Why this gets worse, not better, with scale

Each system is locally correct. The loss happens between them.

-- A supplier is who we pay.
vendor_id   'V-8841'
name        'Nordwind Components GmbH'
country     'DE'   -- billing
status      'A'
-- A supplier is who makes the part.
mfr_ref     'NORDWIND-PL2'
plant_city  'Brno'       -- actual
country     'CZ'        -- manufacturing
approved    true

Both are right. Purchasing needs the legal entity it pays; engineering needs the plant that made the part. One company, two plants, two countries, and one word doing both jobs.

  • Join them on name and you get a plausible answer that is wrong exactly when it matters, which is when a specific plant is sanctioned.
  • Nobody erred. The distinction was never written down.
Say this one out loud

Integration failures are almost never bad data. They are two correct models of the world meeting with no record of how they differ. In Session 3 these become two classes; in Session 5 you decide, with a number attached, whether they are the same thing.

Check question

Two systems store suppliers. One column is vendor_id, the other supplier_code, both strings. An engineer maps them one to one and the join succeeds on 80 percent of rows. What has been established?

The habit this question is training

Separate what the data shows from what you decided it means. Every hour of this course lives on that line.

A modelling choice, not a vendor preference

Two graph models. Only one of them can carry a schema you can reason over.

# subject   predicate      object
ul:ship-4471 ul:handledBy ul:carrier-dhl .
ul:ship-4471 rdf:type     ul:LateShipment .

# The schema is data too, in the same graph:
ul:LateShipment rdfs:subClassOf ul:Shipment .
  • Global names, so two organisations merge graphs without a meeting.
  • The vocabulary is itself queryable. That is what makes OWL and SHACL possible.
// Nodes and edges both carry properties
(s:Shipment {id:'4471', late:true})
  -[:HANDLED_BY {cost: 812}]->
(c:Carrier {name:'DHL'})

// The label 'Shipment' is a string.
// Nothing anywhere defines what it means.
  • Edge properties without ceremony, and a friendlier query language.
  • No standard schema language, so no reasoning and nothing published to reuse.
Why this course is RDF-native

Ontology reuse, SHACL validation and reasoning are the substance of this course, and none of the three exist in the property graph world.

The most expensive misunderstanding in this room

Drag the seam. Same data, same question, two different truths.

Q: Does shipment 4471 have a carrier?

The table has no row for it.

ANSWER: No. It has no carrier.

Absence of data means false.
Q: Does shipment 4471 have a carrier?

The graph has no triple for it.

ANSWER: Unknown. Nobody has said.

Absence of data means unknown.
Why open world is the right default here
  • No single system holds all the facts about a supplier.
  • Merging a partner's graph adds knowledge without invalidating yours.
What it costs you, and who pays it back
  • A reasoner never reports a missing required value. It infers, it does not complain, so OWL cannot be your validator.
  • supplies the closed-world half, in Session 4.

Part three of three · 15 minutes

What it is worth, and which tools are actually alive

Two things a textbook will not tell you: where the money is, and which famous library has not shipped a commit since 2025.

The business case, stated in the terms a business states it in

Four questions, each worth more than the project costs

01 · Traceability

Which products contain a part from this plant?

A recall or sanction turns this into a legal deadline. Hours instead of weeks is the difference between a targeted recall and a total one.

02 · Supplier risk

If this supplier fails, what has no second source?

Walks the bill of materials to arbitrary depth. The query SQL is worst at and a graph is best at.

03 · Late delivery

Which shipment slips next, and why this one?

Prediction is Session 6. The why is what a graph adds: the path through supplier, lane and carrier that explains it.

04 · Cost of a wrong answer

What does a confident wrong answer cost?

More than no answer. Session 7 grades whether your system can say it does not know.

Keep this framing

Nobody funds an ontology. They fund the answer to question 1 with a deadline attached. Learn to describe your work as the second thing.

The transferable skill

Check the last commit, the last release on the package index, and whether the pins still install. A confident README is not evidence.

A fresh version number on a frozen stack. AmpliGraph released in July 2026 and pins TensorFlow 2.15 and Python 3.11 or lower. It looks alive and will not install beside anything current, so you lose the first hour of a lab and conclude you did something wrong. Licences hide the same way: one popular vocabulary in this space carries two contradictory licence statements in the same repository, and you meet it in Session 3.

Inside the lab · worked method

Put the method in the order you will actually work in.

  • Write the rule as one sentence, with the evidence beside it
  • Read the column names and write what you think each one means
  • Hunt for a row that breaks it, and decide whether the row or the rule is wrong
  • Profile: cardinalities, nulls, ranges, value distributions
  • Find a relationship the data always obeys that no schema states
A worked example, end to end

Step 2 shows plant_port has 41 distinct pairs while plant times port would allow 200. Step 3: a plant only ships through ports it is linked to. Step 4: written down with the 41 against 200 as evidence. Step 5: three orders violate it, and they turn out to be transhipments, so the rule was wrong, not the rows.

Because finding a rule feels like finishing, and hunting for a counter-example feels like undoing your own work. It is the opposite: a rule you have attacked and that survived is worth ten you merely observed.

It also decides where the rule goes later. A rule with no exceptions becomes a SHACL shape with severity Violation. A rule with real exceptions becomes a Warning, or a class distinction in the ontology. That decision starts here, in Session 1.

Discussion · 9 minutes · which stage failed?

Three failures. For each one, name the stage of the architecture that broke.

Case A

A recall notice went to 400,000 customers because the only available query was "every product made in that quarter". The specific plant could not be traced through the bill of materials.

Mapping and integration. The bill of materials existed in PLM and was never connected to shipment records, so the multi-hop path did not exist to be walked.

Case B

A dashboard reported 12 percent of shipments as late. The operations team knew the real figure was closer to 30 percent. Both numbers were computed correctly.

The ontology, or rather its absence. Two teams meant different things by "late": against the committed date, or against the customer's requested date. Neither definition was written anywhere, so neither was wrong.

Case C

An assistant answered a supplier question fluently and confidently. The supplier had been delisted eight months earlier. Nobody caught it for a week.

The access layer, and specifically the missing refusal path. The system had no way to say the data was stale or that it did not know, which is graded explicitly in Session 7.

Then, for the remaining minutes

Compare inventories in pairs. Count the rules only one of you found. That number is the argument for writing meaning down.

Wrap

One sentence to leave with.

Integration does not fail because the data is dirty. It fails because two correct models of the world met and nobody wrote down how they differed.
